# Narrative Architecture: Canonical Deck Structures

The deck is not a document. It is an argument delivered in a fixed order, under
time pressure, to a distracted reader. This file holds the canonical structures
from the funds and datasets, the stage deltas, and the narrative rules that
survive across all of them.

Sources cited as `[n]` → [`sources-and-attribution.md`](sources-and-attribution.md).

---

## 1. The Canonical Structures (Eight Sources)

### 1.1 Sequoia — "Writing a Business Plan" [2]

The oldest and most widely copied structure. Ten sections, in this order:

| # | Section | Sequoia's own words |
| :-- | :--- | :--- |
| 1 | Company purpose | *"Define your company in a single declarative sentence. This is harder than it looks. It's easy to get caught up listing features instead of communicating your mission."* |
| 2 | Problem | *"Describe the pain of your customer. How is this addressed today and what are the shortcomings to current solutions."* |
| 3 | Solution | *"Explain your eureka moment. Why is your value prop unique and compelling? Why will it endure? And where does it go from here?"* |
| 4 | **Why now?** | *"The best companies almost always have a clear why now? Nature hates a vacuum—so why hasn't your solution been built before now?"* |
| 5 | Market potential | *"Identify your customer and your market. Some of the best companies invent their own markets."* |
| 6 | Competition / alternatives | *"Who are your direct and indirect competitors. Show that you have a plan to win."* |
| 7 | Business model | *"How do you intend to thrive?"* |
| 8 | Team | *"Tell the story of your founders and key team members."* |
| 9 | Financials | *"If you have any, please include."* |
| 10 | Vision | *"If all goes well, what will you have built in five years?"* |

**Load-bearing observation:** Sequoia's own framing is that *"it wasn't really the
slides we liked—it was their ideas, the clarity of their thinking, and the scope of
their ambition"* [2]. The template is a container for thinking quality. Copying the
sections without the thinking produces a deck that looks right and reads empty.

**Version warning.** The widely circulated **2015 SlideShare version** of Sequoia's
template had a different order and included a separate **Product** section. The
current published order above **replaces Product with Vision** and adds
"Competition / alternatives" and "Why now?" as named sections. Most third-party
guides still reproduce the superseded 2015 order. Use the current order, and do
not tell a founder that Sequoia requires a Product slide — the current template
does not.

### 1.2 YC seed template (Aaron Harris) [1]

Eleven slides. YC's stated principle: *"founders should strive for clarity and
concision… The simple truth is that there isn't very much meaningful detail to
explore for most seed stage companies. When founders pretend that there is, their
stories get muddled, and the investors get lost. **Focus on narrative. The rest is
commentary.**"*

| # | Slide | Notes from the source |
| :-- | :--- | :--- |
| 1 | Title | Company name + one-line description |
| 2 | Problem | *"clearly state the problem. Particulars of how this problem impacts real world people/businesses are valuable."* |
| 3 | Solution | *"explain what you do very clearly, in as few words as possible. Describe the concrete benefits."* |
| 4 | Traction | *"Show off your traction (if you have it). Make the numbers clear and meaningful."* |
| 5 | More metrics | *"Trick slide! Revenue would be better here, but this is ok."* |
| 6 | Insight / why you win | *"Tell the investor what makes you so special, what makes this work, what your insights are. This might take more than one slide."* |
| 7 | Business model | *"You probably don't know all the details yet, but you should know a lot of them. Lay it out."* |
| 8 | Market | *"Is it going to be big? Will you make it big? Convince the investor that they're going to make lots of money with you."* |
| 9 | Team | *"So important at seed. Talk about what makes your team particularly well suited to the problem. **This should be about founders. Nobody cares about your advisors.**"* |
| 10 | The ask | *"Tell the investor how much money you need, and what it gets you. If you can lay out where you'll be inside of a year, which should make you Series A ready, that's powerful."* |

**Slide-set rule (YC-specific):** the title slide is the *only* slide limited to
one page. Every other topic may be a set — *"if you can keep the set to n=1, that's
ideal, but if you need more, that's ok. You probably don't want any set here where
n > 3."* [1]

**Ordering disagreement to flag:** YC's seed template places **traction before
market size** and puts **team late**. Sequoia places **market before competition
and team**, with **no traction section at all**. Both are correct for their
audience: YC optimises for evidence of demand at seed; Sequoia's template predates
the modern seed round and optimises for a complete business argument.

### 1.3 DocSend seed benchmark structure [5]

Derived from tracking how long investors actually spent per section. Target deck
length: **19–20 pages**. Average total read time: **3 minutes 44 seconds** (current page; an older version of the same page said 3 min 20 s). Only **58% of decks are viewed to completion** [5].

| # | Section | Target pages | Industry avg pages | VC time spent |
| :-- | :--- | :-- | :-- | :-- |
| 1 | Company purpose | 1 | 1.3 | 26 s |
| 2 | Problem | 1–2 | 2.15 | 34 s |
| 3 | Solution | 1–2 | 1.5 | 34 s |
| 4 | Market size | 1–3 | 1.7 | 29 s |
| 5 | Why now | 1 | 1.5 | 23 s |
| 6 | Product | 3–5 | 3.3 | **59 s** |
| 7 | Competition | 1 | 1.3 | 34 s |
| 8 | Traction | 1–4 | 2.3 | 40 s |
| 9 | Team | 1–2 | 1.5 | 38 s |
| 10 | Business model | 2–3 | 2.8 | **64 s** |
| 11 | Financials | 1–2 | 1.4 | 37 s |
| 12 | The ask | 1 | 1.2 | 32 s |

**Ordering finding:** decks that opened with the first four sections — purpose,
problem, solution, market size — raised money at a higher rate than decks using
other orders [5].

**Attention finding:** the two sections that consume the most investor time are
**product** and **business model**. Decks that skimp on those two while padding
team and vision are optimising the wrong slides.

### 1.4 Techstars — the deliberate anti-template [12]

Techstars publishes the opposite advice to everyone else, and it is worth taking
seriously because it attacks the failure mode this skill's AP-01 describes:

> *"A lot of people make the mistake of it being a paint-by-numbers exercise that
> something on the internet told them. Here's what 10 slides you should have."*

Techstars' prescription:
1. **Write the long-form script first**, then build slides to fit the story.
2. **No fixed slide order** — *"Sometimes the team should be first."*
3. Anchor on two things: a **big future vision** and **concrete recent momentum**,
   where momentum is explicitly *not* only revenue: *"When we say momentum, we
   don't always mean revenue and user traction."* Their example of good
   specificity: *"In 87 days, they've integrated six services… tracking 500,000
   artists… 130 million fan interactions."*
4. **Text density rule**: *"People will listen, or they will read. They will not do
   both."* Dense content goes to the appendix.

**Implication:** a deck that follows no template but has a script, a vision, and
dated momentum is not thereby deficient. The audit scores criteria, not
compliance with a structure — and `AC-28` (appendix) plus `AC-13` (momentum) carry
the Techstars standard.

### 1.5 500 Global — the formulaic 10-slide template [13]

500 Global publishes a free template and its own ten-section order, and is
unusually explicit that *"we typically only spend a few minutes reviewing each
deck"* and that a deck *"doesn't need more than 10 slides"*.

Order: logo + elevator pitch · problem · solution · how it works · traction ·
business model · competition · market opportunity · **progress to date** · team.

Two reusable copy formulas:
- *"A [product type] to help [target customer] with [#1 problem] by [#1 benefit]
  using our [secret sauce/differentiator]."*
- *"Unlike [existing alternatives], [your product] [primary differentiator] and
  [secondary differentiator]."*

500 Global also imposes a hard market floor: **> $1B**, or a credible expansion
plan to reach it. This is stricter than Sequoia (*"some of the best companies
invent their own markets"* [2]) and is a genuine disagreement, not a nuance.

**Anti-pattern they name:** *"saying you're the 'first' to do something often
shows a lack of knowledge about your space."*

### 1.6 DocSend pre-seed benchmark structure [4]

Target deck length: **18 pages**. Average read time: **4 minutes 10 seconds** —
*longer* than seed, because there is more explaining to do and less evidence to do
it with.

| # | Section | Target pages | VC time spent |
| :-- | :--- | :-- | :-- |
| 1 | Company purpose | 1 | 33 s |
| 2 | Problem | 1–2 | 39 s |
| 3 | Solution | 1–2 | 27 s |
| 4 | Why now | 1 | 38 s |
| 5 | Product | 3–4 | **77 s** |
| 6 | Market size | 1 | 39 s |
| 7 | Team | 1–2 | 46 s |
| 8 | Business model | 2–3 | **83 s** |
| 9 | Traction | 1–4 | 37 s |
| 10 | Financials | 1–2 | 40 s |
| 11 | Competition | 1 | 55 s |
| 12 | The ask | 1 | 40 s |

**Pre-seed deltas vs seed:** product moves *earlier* (position 5 vs 6) and gets
more time when it is all you have; team moves *earlier* (position 7 vs 9) because
at pre-seed the bet is on the person; competition moves *later* (position 11 vs 7).

### 1.7 YC Series A deck [7]

The Series A deck is a *proof* artifact, not a *promise* artifact. YC's framing:
*"at the Series A, you need to have data to show you've made good on the promises
you made at the seed."*

| # | Slide | Notes from the source |
| :-- | :--- | :--- |
| 1 | One-liner | *"We help people quit smoking. Our product is so good that in just the past 2 years, we've reached 500k WAU growing at 20% m/m."* |
| 2 | Problem | 1 slide. Present it **from the paying customer's perspective**, and *"show, not tell"* — describe how the world works today concretely |
| 3 | Solution | 1 slide. **Side-by-side comparison** against the status quo, with quantified impact. *"Focus on what you do right now, not what you plan to do"* |
| 4 | Traction | **Several slides.** Revenue first if you have it, then acquisition, then engagement/retention |
| 5 | Market | 1 slide. **Bottoms-up**: *"number of prospective customers x value of each customer to you"* |
| 6 | Competition | 1 slide. *"Show why you're 10x better… convince the investor that you have a high enough moat"* |
| 7 | Vision | 1 slide. *"Show how you become a $10B company"* |
| 8 | Team | 1 slide. Move to slide 2–3 **if the team is a comparative advantage** |
| 9 | Use of Funds | 1 slide. *"The ask is the climax of your whole deck"* |
| 10 | Appendix | As many slides as needed; answers the Q&A list |

**Quantified requirements YC states directly:**
- **At least 4–6 months of trend** before growth is believable [7].
- A monthly or quarterly graph beats a summed annual number [7].
- **Never use cumulative numbers** (*"almost every time I've seen cumulative
  numbers in a Series A deck, it's because founders are trying to hide their
  monthly/quarterly numbers"*) or **double-axis graphs** [7].
- Team slide: *"Remove anything else — yes, this means leaving off the headshots
  for every employee in your 12 person team"* [7].
- Design: *"Optimize for clarity and understanding, not beauty… keep it as simple
  and bare-bones as possible"* [7].

### 1.8 YC demo-day / presented deck [3]

A different artifact with different physics: **5–7 slides**, **2 minutes 30
seconds**, 500+ people in the room, investors checking email.

Rules from Kevin Hale:
- **Legible** — large type, bold, simple font, good contrast, text at the top.
- **Simple** — one idea per slide; *"simple and complex share the same word
  origin… simple ideas are one fold."*
- **Obvious** — understood at a glance; *"show it to a stranger and ask them to
  tell you what it means. If they don't immediately say your idea, you lose."*
- **Explicit** — write the conclusion on the slide. Diagrams are *"little mazes
  for ideas"*; the path to the idea should be straight and quick.
- **Remove:** too much text, excessive explanations and caveats, excessive branding
  per slide, photos without captions, animations, transitions, memes, subtle
  humour, accidental humour.
- **Screenshots:** usually hostile to all three rules — *"they are the worst"* —
  unless cropped and captioned.

**The reconciliation rule:** the presented deck and the read-ahead deck are two
exports of one narrative. Sending the demo-day 6-slide deck as a read-ahead is as
wrong as presenting the 20-page read-ahead. See P-11.

### 1.9 The unresolved length disagreement

Funds say **10 slides**; the tracking data says **18–20 pages**. This is the single
largest unresolved conflict in the corpus, and this skill does not pretend to
settle it.

| Source | Length | Evidence tier |
| :--- | :--- | :--- |
| YC demo day, on stage | **5–7 slides**, 2 min 30 s | fund's own words [3] |
| YC seed | ~10–11 sections, no section > 3 slides | fund's own words [1] |
| YC Series A | ~9 named slides + appendix "as many as necessary" | fund's own words [7] |
| Antler, pre-seed | **10 slides** | fund's own words [8] |
| 500 Global | **≤ 10 slides** | fund's own words [13] |
| Techstars | no fixed count; script-first | fund's own words [12] |
| DocSend, pre-seed | **18 pages**, 12 sections | tracking dataset [4] |
| DocSend, seed | **19–20 pages**, 12 sections | tracking dataset [5] |
| Papermark, 3,000 decks (2024) | **9–16 pages** = 49% of decks | independent dataset [10] |
| Sequoia | publishes no number | — |

**The most plausible reconciliation is sections vs slides** — an 18-page deck of
~10 content sections plus dividers, appendix, and title. **No source states this**,
so the skill treats it as a hypothesis and reports both numbers. Never tell a
founder that a fund requires 20 slides, or that 10 slides is a rule.

## 2. The Universal Narrative Arc

Every canonical structure above reduces to the same five-beat arc. The sections
are surface; the arc is load-bearing.

```
BEAT 1 — TENSION      A specific, quantified pain, with a named who.
BEAT 2 — TURN         The dated change that makes solving it possible now.
BEAT 3 — ANSWER       The wedge: what you do, for whom, and why it works.
BEAT 4 — PROOF        Evidence it already works: product, traction, economics.
BEAT 5 — THE BET      Team, ask, and what the money buys.
```

Failure modes map 1:1 onto missing beats:

| Missing beat | What the investor experiences | Anti-pattern |
| :--- | :--- | :--- |
| Tension | "Nobody needs this." | AP-03, AP-05 |
| Turn | "This could have been built in 2019. Why you, why now?" | AP-02 |
| Answer | "I don't know what they do." | AP-01, AP-05, AP-06 |
| Proof | "This is a plan, not a company." | AP-12, AP-18, AP-15 |
| The Bet | "I don't know what they want from me." | AP-30, AP-31 |

## 3. Stage Deltas

| Dimension | Pre-seed | Seed | Series A |
| :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- |
| What is actually being bought | The founders + the insight | Evidence of product-market fit forming | A repeatable, efficient growth machine |
| Lead slide after the one-liner | Problem | Traction | Traction / efficiency |
| Product proof | Prototype, demo, design partners | Live product, usage data | Scale, retention, gross margin |
| Traction expected | Engagement, pilots, LOIs | Revenue or strong usage growth | $1–3M+ ARR, cohort retention, net revenue retention |
| Market argument | Thesis + wedge | Bottom-up sizing + wedge | Bottom-up sizing + expansion path demonstrated |
| Financials | Optional / direction of travel | 12–24 month model | 3-year model with actuals vs plan |
| Unit economics | Modelled, labelled | Early actuals | Actuals with cohort-level detail |
| Competition | Positioning thesis | Positioning + feature reality | Win/loss data |
| Deck length target | ~18 pages [4] | ~19–20 pages [5] | Longer, appendix-heavy |
| Read time | ~4 min 10 s [4] | ~3 min 44 s [5] | Longer, but the partner meeting carries it |
| a16z emphasis | Insight + market | Burn multiple + market | Efficiency + rule of 40 [6] |
| YC emphasis | Founder-market fit + problem specificity | Growth rate + traction + team [1] | Retention + unit economics + trends of 4–6 months [7] |
| Sequoia emphasis | Clarity of thinking + ambition [2] | Clarity of thinking + why now [2] | Market potential + plan to win [2] |

**Rule:** if the deck's stage claim and its evidence disagree, the deck is
inconsistent (AC-25 `FAIL`) regardless of how good the evidence is.

## 4. The Nine Narrative Rules

These hold across all four structures and both artifacts.

1. **One idea per slide.** Two ideas means two slides. [3]
2. **The headline is the takeaway, not the label.** "Market" is a label;
   "38,000 labs must retest weekly under the new rule" is a takeaway.
3. **Every number is sourced or marked as your own estimate.**
4. **Front-load the most compelling verified fact.** Investors decide in the first
   four slides and spend the rest of the read justifying or rejecting that
   decision. DocSend found opening with purpose → problem → solution → market
   correlates with higher raise rates [5].
5. **Why Now is an event with a date, not a trend.** [5]
6. **The ask is a slide, not a sentence you say at the end.** [4][5]
7. **The worst honest fact goes in the deck.** Every investor will find it; the
   deck that names it first controls the frame. See P-13.
8. **Write the takeaway in words next to every chart.** [3]
9. **Detail lives in the appendix, not the flow.** [4][5]

## 5. Slide-by-Slide Assembly Recipe

When authoring (rather than auditing), build in this order. The order matters:
each step constrains the next.

```
Step 0  Collect facts first. Never invent a number. Anything unknown becomes a
        bracketed placeholder and a line in "Numbers to find".
Step 1  Write the one-liner (≤ 15 words). Do not proceed until it passes the
        Party Test.
Step 2  Write the Why Now event with a year and a number.
Step 3  Write the wedge: beachhead segment + why you win there.
Step 4  Write the problem as evidence of the tension (not as a complaint).
Step 5  Write the product story as 3–5 single-idea slides with captioned visuals.
Step 6  Write the proof block: traction, unit economics, cohort/retention.
Step 7  Write competition with the honest losing row.
Step 8  Write the team as achievement lines.
Step 9  Write the ask: size, instrument, committed, close date, use of funds %.
Step 10 Write the vision as the end state of the wedge (not a new idea).
Step 11 Build the appendix from every question you were asked the last time you
        pitched.
Step 12 Cut. Target 10–12 slides presented / 18–20 read-ahead.
```

## 6. Variant Generation (Fund-Specific)

Because the same deck cannot be optimal for a16z, YC, and Sequoia simultaneously
(see [`patterns-and-antipatterns.md`](patterns-and-antipatterns.md) Part 3), the
skill produces **one base narrative plus per-fund variants**.

A variant changes only:
- **Slide order** (e.g. YC seed: traction before market; Sequoia: market before
  competition)
- **Emphasis and depth** (e.g. a16z: expand the market/insight and efficiency
  slides; YC: expand traction and team)
- **The framing sentence** on the title and closing slides
- **Which appendix slides** are promoted into the main flow

A variant must **never** change a number, add a claim, or drop a Critical
acceptance criterion. If a fund's emphasis set cannot be satisfied without
inventing evidence, the correct output is a gap report, not a variant.
